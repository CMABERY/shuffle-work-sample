What happens when a player reaches a stake limit?

Completed simulation and interactive review.

I compared stake limits across 2,500 simulated Dice sessions. Each player started with $100 and staked 5% of their balance, subject to the cap, for up to 10,000 bets.

The question was how exposure changes when a player accepts a smaller bet—or leaves after a refusal.

Peak profit means the player’s highest profit above their starting deposit during a session. Figures are rounded to the nearest dollar.

Only 35 sessions were affected by the tighter cap. Continuing at the lower stake reduced the largest peak, but slightly increased the 99th percentile. Assuming those players left produced a different result again.

These are results under a stated model. They do not measure Shuffle customers or establish a recommended live limit. Actual player response is the missing evidence.

Turn that uncertainty into something measurable

Working tool; synthetic demonstration data.

I built a request-history analyzer to examine what happens after a player encounters a cap. It distinguishes subsequent accepted play, play under a different recorded cap, and an observation window with no subsequent accepted request. Incomplete records remain marked incomplete.

In the example, changing the window from five to thirty minutes reveals later play that the shorter window misses. You can inspect the events behind the classification.

Open the investigation. Applying it to Shuffle would require mapping an actual export and checking its coverage. The bundled examples are constructed cases; they do not estimate how often customers behave this way.

Check the premise, too

Historical account records.

I began by trying to find an advantage in Shuffle.us Dice. I searched 2,988 candidate patterns in my history. None passed the declared development-and-holdout standard. This was a retrospective study; it did not rule out every small effect.

Using my own Shuffle.us account history, I regenerated 25,054 revealed rolls from their recorded seeds. All matched. That verifies those recorded outcomes against the replay calculation; it does not establish how seeds were selected or what was committed before each bet.

A first project: one promotion

Kingside’s sweepstakes CRM listing names wagering requirements, bonus economics and promotion leakage. I’d start with one offer and its intended behavior, agreed with the team.

Over two weeks, I propose to deliver:

  • A runnable model of the rules, with ambiguities and assumptions documented.
  • Comparisons of ordinary play, simple automated strategies and AI-assisted strategy search where useful, measuring completion rates and the distribution of player returns and promotion costs.
  • A short recommendation: which changes matter, their trade-offs for legitimate players, and what evidence would validate the findings.

The first pass would use agreed rules and synthetic play. Finding that an offer withstands the tested strategies would also be a useful result.
